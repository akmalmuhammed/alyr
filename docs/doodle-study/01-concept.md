# Doodle study: concept

28 Oct 2026, Site and brand lane. This is a prototype for the founder in Framer ("ALYR homepage
prototypes"). Nothing is published.

The founder asked for an animated doodle-art background for the homepage, "that suits well
with alyr", whose story "subtly should showcase how alyr helps small business". The art sits
behind the live hero: "The tech team your business never had.", its lead and "Book a check-up".

## What kind of doodle fits ALYR

ALYR's look is "a working drawing of an owner's business, made by an engineer who respects
the owner's time" (`alyr-design` §1). So the right doodle is a drawing from an architect's or
engineer's sketchbook, not a cartoon.

**What fits:**
- **A single-weight technical-pen line.** One stroke width everywhere, with square ends and no
  fills. Shading, where it is needed, is hatching.
- **Ruled by hand, not by machine.** Long lines waver by less than a pixel, and corners cross
  and overshoot by 2 to 3 px, as an architect's sketch does. This is where the hand-drawn feel
  comes from. The paths themselves are irregular; there are no filters.
- **Line boil.** Each line has three slightly different takes, swapped at 8 frames a second,
  so the drawing breathes the way hand-drawn animation does. No take moves more than 0.6 px.
- **One continuous line.** The ALYR thread is one unbroken stroke from start to finish. It
  is the only line drawn while you watch, and its turns are cut at 45° (the Command Fold's
  gesture, as in the "Then we check again" return line).
- **Sketchbook margins.** The drawing is dense at the far side and along the bottom edge, and
  thins to empty paper where the words are. Ink is faint (`--hair-strong`, 14% Carbon on
  Paper, 13% chalk on Carbon), so it reads as a page behind the page.
- **Chaos is round, order is cut.** The loose messages are wobbly, rounded scribbles. What
  ALYR straightens them into has square edges and one 45° cut corner, the brand's shape.

**What to avoid:**
- clip-art clutter, a busy all-over pattern or a "doodle wallpaper";
- mascots, faces, hands, cartoon people;
- emoji, stars, hearts or sparkles (sparkles are also AI imagery, which the brand bans);
- faux-3D, drop shadows, gradients, glow or texture overlays;
- generic tech doodles: rockets, gears, lightbulbs, clouds, robots, brains, charts with
  arrows shooting up;
- WhatsApp's logo, ticks or green. The message bubbles are plain shapes;
- local-colour clichés the brand already rules out (`alyr-design` §1.6: no skylines, dhows,
  coffee pots or arabesque) and anything that is a real brand, shop sign or landmark.

## Three directions

**A. One line through the street.** A faint row of small shops (a bakery, a salon, a
home-services van) at the bottom far side of the hero. Morning: the shutters roll up and a
phone fills with tangled messages. One continuous line, the ALYR thread, enters along the
street and puts things in order. It ends back where it started.
- For: it tells the whole story (check, set up, see, improve) without a word, and it is about
  the owner's world, not about software.
- Against: it is the most drawing, so it is the most at risk of clutter.

**B. The counter, close up.** One shop counter drawn large, as a still life: a phone, a
notebook of who owes what, a ticket rail, a receipt printer and a cup. The thread works only
on this counter.
- For: it is the calmest of the three and the easiest to keep honest.
- Against: it is one trade, and it reads as a product illustration more than a background. It
  loses the sense of a street of businesses ALYR serves.

**C. The working drawing.** The shop as an architect's plan and elevation, with dimension
lines and section crosses. The ALYR thread is routed through the plan like a cable, and
labels tick as it passes.
- For: it is closest to the brand's "precision industrial minimalism".
- Against: it is cold. It explains a system instead of showing an owner's day, and it needs
  words (labels) to work.

## Chosen: A, drawn with C's hand and B's restraint

The recommended direction is right about the story. I changed five things, each for a reason.

1. **Drawn as an architect's sketch** (from C): a ruled line with overshooting corners instead
   of a loose cartoon line. That is what makes a doodle read as ALYR rather than a
   stationery pattern.
2. **Three shops, which are the site's three sample trades.** These are a bakery (the
   croissant sign), a salon (the mirror) and a home-services van. The site's samples are
   Qirfa Bakery, Dafira Salon and Zilal Cooling, so the street is ALYR's own world. No names
   are drawn, so there is nothing to caption and no name to check.
3. **No barjeel, no palm and no figure.** The brand bans skylines and local-colour clichés,
   and the wind tower and palm are the first things a stock "Doha" illustration reaches for.
   `alyr-design` §1.6 also says no people. The owner's calm moment is shown by objects
   instead: the phone goes quiet and steam rises from a karak glass on the counter. The karak
   glass is everyday Doha, not heritage decoration, but it is the one local touch, so the
   founder should confirm it [F].
4. **Order arrives with the cut corner.** The scribbled bubbles become order tickets on a
   kitchen-style ticket rail. Each ticket is square with one 45° cut. The notebook's lines get
   ticks, the salon's week fills slot by slot, and a short receipt prints with a hand-lettered
   "#1001". (The site's first order is #1001.)
5. **The week is shown as a comparison, not a result.** Two small bars rise: last week as an
   outline, this week as an outline with one Volt mark at its top (the brand's "one signal",
   today's mark). There are no numbers, no arrow and no labels. This week's bar is a little
   taller. That still suggests ALYR improves sales, so it is on the founder's list [F].

**Volt** is used in two places, one item at a time:
- **the thread's tip:** the current state. On Paper it is a Volt dot with a Carbon ring,
  because a thin Volt line has no contrast on light paper;
- **the thing just fixed:** one tick, one ticket's edge, one slot, the receipt or the week's
  mark. On Paper this is a short Volt highlighter stroke under the ink, like a marker in a
  sketchbook; on Carbon it is the Volt line itself. It passes to the next thing when the next
  thing is fixed. There is never more than one at a time.

**The loop (24 s, one day):**

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 to 1.6 s | Morning. Three shutters roll up. |
| 1.6 to 5 s | The phone on the bakery counter fills with scribbled bubbles, "?" marks and a voice-note squiggle that drift up into a loose tangle. The notebook on the counter is a mess. |
| 5 to 6.5 s | The thread enters along the street from the text side, its Volt tip leading. |
| 6.5 to 10 s | The thread lassoes the tangle, and the bubbles straighten one by one into tickets on the rail. |
| 10 to 12 s | The thread ticks the notebook's three lines and prints a receipt. |
| 12 to 15 s | The thread crosses to the salon, and the week fills slot by slot. |
| 15 to 17 s | The thread passes the van, whose job card ticks, and climbs to draw the two bars. |
| 17 to 21 s | Calm. Steam rises from the glass, and the phone shows one neat line. |
| 21 to 23 s | The thread runs back along the street to where it started, closing the loop. This echoes "Then we check again". |
| 23 to 24 s | Evening. Everything fades back, and the shutters come down for the next morning. |

**Motion rules, adapted.** `motion.md` says "plays once". The founder's D-059 ("always on")
allows figures to keep moving, and this one follows its conditions:
- it plays only while on screen and in a visible tab;
- it has a Pause button ("Pause" / «إيقاف», named "Pause the drawing" / «إيقاف حركة الرسم»);
- it is a finished still frame with reduced motion and on Framer's canvas.

**Arabic** mirrors the whole drawing, so the thread runs right to left and the words sit on
the right. The "#1001" is drawn unmirrored, and time in the bars runs right to left.
