# ALYR films: creative direction

Oct 24, 2026 · @akmal

## The direction on one page

Make one 90-second hero film, "The other job", and six 30-second product films on one shared system. Start with the ALYR Chat film, made entirely in code, while the hero is storyboarded, reviewed and shot.

- **The one idea:** ALYR takes the other job. It comes to your shop, sets your business up as one system, and keeps improving it with you.
- **The line:** "You have two jobs. We take one." It hands over to the homepage's headline, "The tech team your business never had.", and its one action, "Book a check-up".
- **The story:** Huda, owner of Qirfa Bakery, still answering chats at 23:40. Her scatter cuts across a salon and an AC company too. Then the visit: her business drawn as dashed lines, which turn solid as one order travels Shelf, Chat, Accounts, Docs and Agent. Sunday, her week on one screen. One change made together. At 21:10 she switches off the light and goes home.
- **The look and sound:** things arrive along the 45° diagonal, settle, and one Volt tick confirms them. Live action carries the feeling; real screens made in code carry the product. Sound is written first, with one silence a film.
- **What changes from the brief to stay true today:** the hero's order goes on account instead of a card payment; the Sunday report appears with its condition; statements are dropped; ALYR Docs shows receipts; no film says "your number" or "live".
- **How it gets made:** one live-action shoot day in Doha for the hero and the product films' openings; everything else in code; voice actors and a composer commissioned.
- **What I need from you:** the ten questions at the end, starting with people on screen, the line, and the Arabic voice.

Only the Set up act is made entirely in code; it is where the product is shown, and where the score builds one product at a time.

## What I learned

The films that work for small businesses give the owner a problem with a clock on it, solve one beat of it per product moment, and end on a line, not a feature list. The twenty principles below are the ones these films use, each tied to a film or source we confirmed exists.

**How this was studied.** Every film and page below was opened and confirmed (the watch page, the studio's case study, trade press, award listings). Nobody on this work watched the films with sound; the craft notes come from directors' interviews, studio case studies and press that describe the films. Where a source is weak, it says so.

**Corrections to the brief:**

- Slack's film is from 2014, and it is about Sandwich, a real video studio, not a fictional company.
- Shopify's "Let's Make You a Business" is from 2019.
- These could not be confirmed and are not cited: Stripe Sessions opening films (the openings are live keynotes); Linear "Asks" and "Insights" launch films; Notion Mail and Arc Search launch films; an Apple film by Ordinary Folk; Giant Ant work for Stripe or Google.
- Apple has made its own uploads of "The Underdogs" private; the working copies are crew uploads on Vimeo.

## Three hero concepts

I recommend **A, "The other job"**, with C's working drawing as its middle act. Each concept below is judged on one test: after one viewing, can an owner say in one sentence what ALYR does?

### A. The other job (the brief's idea, developed)

**Logline.** Every owner has two jobs, the one they opened the shop for and the one nobody sees; ALYR comes to the shop, sets up the second, and keeps improving it.

**Treatment.** We open at 23:40 on three owners in three shops, each lit by a phone: a baker, a salon owner, an AC company owner. The pings of the day speed up into a rhythm as match cuts join their scatter: a notebook, an Excel sheet, forty unread chats, an empty chair, a name in the debt book. Silence. Daylight: a visitor from ALYR sits at the baker's counter and draws her business as a working drawing, every connection a dashed line. Then, one by one, the dashed lines turn solid as each part is set up, and a single order travels the drawing from Shelf to Agent. Sunday 09:00, her week on one screen. A short talk across the counter, one change made, and the arc loops back to Check. The film ends where it began, at closing time, but now the owner switches off the light and goes home before midnight. Line: "You have two jobs. We take one." Headline: "The tech team your business never had."

**Strengths.** The feeling the audience already has ("I do it at night") is the hook; the visit makes ALYR people, not software; the ending is a change you can see (the light goes off at a sane hour).

**Risks.** It needs a small live-action shoot. "We take one" must stay true: the film shows ALYR answering the everyday requests and counting, and shows the owner still pressing the buttons that matter (confirm, send reminders).

### B. Big team, one owner

**Logline.** A big company has consultants, an IT team, a finance desk and a reception; a small shop has one owner who is all of them, until ALYR.

**Treatment.** A split screen, 45° down the middle. On one side, a corporate floor: a consultant at a whiteboard, an IT desk, a finance team, a receptionist. On the other, the baker, alone, playing every role in turn as the cuts mirror each other exactly: the whiteboard against her notebook, the IT desk against her phone, the finance team against her calculator. At the turn, the 45° line folds (the brand's fold), and each corporate role collapses into one ALYR product on her single screen. Line: "Big businesses have a team for this. You have us."

**Strengths.** States the positioning literally, and matches the homepage's "Why ALYR" table word for word; the mirrored edit is very satisfying.

**Risks.** Two sets and a larger cast cost more. The corporate side can read as a joke at big companies' expense, and the owner spends the film as a punchline. The payoff is a comparison, not a feeling.

### C. Dashed to solid

**Logline.** At the check-up, the owner's business is drawn as a set of dashed lines; the film is those lines turning solid, one at a time, until the drawing is a business that runs as one thing.

**Treatment.** Paper and Carbon only, nearly all made from code and real screens. A notebook page with Qirfa Bakery's business drawn on it: customers, the chat, the price list, the oven, the debt book, the week. Every link is dashed, every node an object (a phone, a notebook, a calculator). As the voice names a problem, the node shakes loose; as ALYR sets it up, the dashed line draws solid along 45° and the object becomes its real screen at true scale, settling with one Volt tick. One order runs the solid lines end to end. The finished drawing folds into the ALYR mark.

**Strengths.** It is the brand's own grammar (dashed means coming, solid means true) turned into story; it is cheap, fast, fully honest, and every product film can reuse it.

**Risks.** It is clever rather than felt. Without a person, the audience admires it and forgets it.

### Why A, with C inside it

A wins the test: an owner leaves able to say "they come to my shop, set it all up as one thing, and keep improving it." C becomes A's 35-second middle act and the visual system of all six product films, so the series shares one look. B is not wasted: its line, "Big businesses have a team for this. You have us.", is the strongest 15-second social idea, made in code as a mirrored split screen (see the 6-second bumpers under Formats and cut-downs).

## The hero film: "The other job" (90 s)

**The one idea:** ALYR takes the other job: it comes to your shop, sets your business up as one system, and keeps improving it with you. **What the owner should say after one viewing:** "They come to my shop, set it all up as one thing, and keep making it better."

**Who we follow:** Huda, the owner of Qirfa Bakery (the name the ALYR Agent samples already greet). Lamees (Dafira Salon) and Adel (Zilal Cooling) appear only in the scatter, so the series has its three businesses from the first film. **The shape:** live action holds the feeling (night, the visit, the light going off); code holds the product (every screen is real, at true scale). The join between them is always a match cut on a line.

## Six product films (30 s each)

Every product film runs the same six beats, so the series reads as one system and any film can be cut into any other:

1. **The pain** (0–3 s): one object, one sound, live action or a still.
2. **The friction** (3–8 s): the moment it goes wrong.
3. **The fix** (8–20 s): the real screen at true scale, one action at a time.
4. **The hand-off** (20–24 s): what this product passes to the next one, shown as the line carrying on.
5. **The result** (24–27 s): what the owner now sees in ALYR Agent, with its rule or why line.
6. **The end card** (27–30 s): the product's name, its one line from the site, "Part of ALYR", "Book a check-up".

The businesses rotate so the series covers all three: Chat and Shelf at Qirfa Bakery, Bookings at Dafira Salon, Accounts and Docs at Zilal Cooling, and Agent across all three. All customer and business names are the samples' own; the ones still marked "not checked" in the names register (Ward Events, Bayview School) need the founder's check before release.

## The series system

Every ALYR film, from a 6-second bumper to the hero, is built from the same parts, so a viewer who has seen one recognises the next before the logo appears.

### The grammar: arrive, settle, confirm

- **Arrive** along the logo's 45° diagonal, from the bottom inline-start corner (bottom left in English, bottom right in Arabic), on the arrival ease `cubic-bezier(.16, 1, .3, 1)`.
- **Settle** without bounce, overshoot or float. Film timings use the token steps and may breathe longer: 480 ms for a small element, 720 ms for a screen, up to 1.2 s for a headline line; 60 ms between related elements; 0.4 s between headline lines.
- **Confirm** with one Volt tick, seen and heard, only when something is true or done. At most one Volt element is lit in a frame.

### The opening and the close

- **Every film opens cold, on the pain:** one object and one sound, never the logo or the product name. The first frame must work as a still with the sound off ("23:40" and a lit phone; an empty chair under a clock).
- **Every film closes on the fold:** three Carbon planes with the symbol's gaps sweep in along 45°, then the end card. The fold is the only full-frame transition the brand owns, so it is used twice a film at most: into a Carbon chapter, and into the end card.

### Transitions, each with one meaning

| Transition | Means | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Match cut on shape or direction | The same feeling in another shop | The scatter, joining the three businesses |
| The line join | The real world becomes the system | Live action to code: a pen's line becomes the drawn line |
| Dashed to solid | Set up, and now true | Every product arriving; every hand-off |
| Whip along 45° with motion blur | The same moment, somewhere else | Customer's phone to team screen |
| Clock jump (mono) | Time passes, nobody touched anything | Reminders, Sunday 09:00, closing time |
| Morph | One system, many businesses | The four Agent numbers changing between bakery, salon and AC company |
| The fold | A new chapter, or the end | Into Carbon; into the end card |

### The working drawing

Product screens appear as figures on Paper: a faint 12-column grid, hairline rules, a mono FIG caption ending in a tick, the 45° cut corner on drawn phones and windows. Screens are real, in their real words, at true scale: a phone fills 80% of the frame's height, and one action happens at a time. Dashed rules mean a slot waiting to be set up; solid means set up today. Nothing that is not built is ever drawn, dashed or not.

### Type in motion

- Instrument Sans for Latin, IBM Plex Sans Arabic for Arabic, IBM Plex Mono for clocks, product labels and captions.
- Sentence case. Lines rise from a mask, line by line or word group by word group, never letter by letter, and never split in Arabic. The second line of a pair is grey, so the eye reads the first.
- One thought on screen at a time, held for at least its word count ÷ 3 seconds, plus 1.
- Every film works with the sound off: the kinetic type carries the voice-over's meaning. Captions are designed, not added: two lines at most, about 42 characters a line, in the brand's type, inside the platform's safe zone.

### Colour and restraint

Paper first; Carbon for command moments (the night, the turn, the end card). Volt only for the main action, the current state, confirmed progress or one signal, never as a background. No gradients, glow, 3D phones, stock footage, skylines, dunes, camels, falcons or coffee pots. A held frame (2 to 3 seconds of stillness) is used once a film, at the turn.

### Sound identity

| Sound | What it is | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ping | ALYR's own notification sound, never WhatsApp's | The scatter; it accelerates into rhythm |
| The Volt tick | Two dry notes, about 120 ms, bright | Every confirmed step, and only those |
| The fold | A short paper-and-air sweep, panned along the diagonal (mirrored in Arabic) | Chapter changes and the end card |
| The clock | One mono tick | Clock jumps |
| The sonic logo | Three rising notes along the "diagonal", landing on the Volt tick, about 1.2 s | The logo's arrival, every film |
| Silence | Room tone only, 1 to 3 s | Once a film, just before the turn |

**Music by product, so the hero can stack them:** Chat a pulse, Bookings a clock-like pizzicato, Agent a pad, Accounts low keys, Shelf a muted pluck, Docs a soft paper tick. Tempo 100 to 118 BPM. Mixed to −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP. The scores composed in code for the three October films are placeholders; release music and the sonic logo are commissioned (see the production plan).

### The end card

Carbon. The product's name and its one line from the site (or, for the hero, the headline); "Part of ALYR"; the Volt button "Book a check-up"; the logo, its Volt plane arriving last with the sonic logo; alyr.qa; the disclaimer. Held still for 2 to 3 seconds. No price until the founder switches the offer on.

### Rules for every future film

1. One idea a film, said in one sentence before anything is drawn.
2. Open on the pain, close on the fold. Never open on the logo.
3. Show the product doing, at true scale, one action at a time, in its real words.
4. One order (or one booking) carries the story; products are stations on its line, never a list of features.
5. Sound is written before the edit. One silence a film.
6. Every transition means something from the table above, or it is a straight cut.
7. One Volt signal a frame. No decoration.
8. Readable with the sound off, in both languages, each written as itself.
9. Every sample is fictional and captioned once; every claim is true today on `main`.
10. Nothing is published without the founder's go-ahead, and nothing before the CR.

## Arabic as its own film

Each Arabic film is written, voiced, timed and edited as itself, from the same brief; it is never the English film with subtitles. The Arabic in this document is a working draft in Modern Standard Arabic for a native writer to rewrite, and nothing ships before the native review (ALY-66).

**My recommendation for the voice:** the voice-over is spoken in Gulf Arabic, close to Qatari, by a Gulf narrator; everything written on screen (supers, captions, the end card, every claim) stays in Modern Standard Arabic, as on the site. Gulf practitioners advise dialect for marketing and MSA for anything that must read as a commitment ([Translated](https://translated.com/resources/arabic-dialect-localization-choices); vendor opinion, not a study). This departs from the design system's rule, which keeps Gulf Arabic for the owner's own voice, so it needs the founder's yes (open question 5). The fallback is MSA read by a Gulf narrator, as drafted in the hero's script.

**What changes in the Arabic cut:**

- **Direction.** Every diagonal, whip, fold and arrival mirrors: things arrive from the bottom right, and the fold sweeps from the other side. Sound pans mirror with the picture. Clocks, the ▲▼ marks and the logo never mirror.
- **Screens are the Arabic product, not mirrored English.** The drawn phones show the real Arabic screens and messages, right to left, with Western digits, "480 ر.ق" (amount, then «ر.ق»), and product names in Latin, kept whole.
- **Type.** IBM Plex Sans Arabic, never tracked, never in capitals or italics, never split into letters, with the looser Arabic line height. Kinetic type moves by line or by word group. Mono labels switch to Plex Sans Arabic.
- **Timing.** Arabic lines run longer; the edit is re-timed to the Arabic voice-over, not stretched. The 90-second hero may become 95 seconds in Arabic; the cut-downs keep their lengths by trimming picture, not words.
- **Grammar.** ALYR is feminine («تجهّز ALYR»); every product is masculine («يعمل ALYR Bookings»); "Agent" is never translated.
- **One shoot serves both languages.** No live-action shot has synced dialogue, so the same footage cuts both films, and only the voice, type and screens change.
