## Question it answered
Can ALYR sell its subscription to shops in the other GCC countries with only its Qatar registration, what follows it into each country, and which country should come second?

## Key findings
- Yes. No GCC country requires a local company to sell a software subscription delivered online from Qatar. The line is between selling into a country (allowed) and operating in it (staff, a desk or stock there, which needs a local licence). Government and enterprise tenders usually ask for a local registration in practice.
- VAT follows ALYR from the first sale to an unregistered shop, with no threshold for non-residents: UAE 5% (a tax agent may be required), Saudi 15%, Bahrain 10% (local representative needed), Oman 5%. Kuwait has no VAT and none in its 2026 to 2030 fiscal plan. Sales to VAT-registered shops use reverse charge. Thresholds (AED 375,000, SAR 375,000, BHD 37,500, about OMR 38,500) mean most target shops are unregistered.
- Withholding is the biggest practical problem in Saudi: 5% (services) or 15% (royalties; SaaS has no clear ruling), capped at 8% by the Qatar and Saudi treaty in force since 1 January 2025. A small Saudi shop paying QAR 449 a month is unlikely to withhold, file and send a certificate. Oman has 10% withholding on software and service fees. Kuwait may tax Kuwait-source software income (unverified).
- Privacy laws in Saudi (PDPL, enforced since Sept 2024), UAE (PDPL 45/2021), Bahrain (PDPL 30/2018) and Oman (PDPL 6/2022) reach ALYR abroad. None forces local storage for this kind of product. The study recommends one privacy policy and data agreement written to the strictest law, Saudi's.
- E-invoicing mainly binds residents: Saudi exempts non-residents from Fatoora for now; UAE residents phase in from 2027; Oman from April 2027. Saudi's E-Commerce Law covers sellers abroad and asks for clear disclosures.
- The GCC common market helps only if the owners are GCC nationals. A Qatar company owned by non-GCC nationals is an ordinary foreign investor elsewhere. There is no GCC-wide licence, VAT number or payment licence. It raises shareholder nationality at CR as an open question for the founder.
- Payments travel well: each shop is paid by a provider licensed in its own country, so ALYR needs no payment licence anywhere. Tap covers all six countries; MyFatoorah is the reserve everywhere; SADAD is Qatar-only. ALYR's own subscription fee is a separate flow, and local debit networks (KNET, mada) often fail for foreign merchants (unverified).
- WhatsApp is not a reason to register locally. Meta prices by the recipient's country: Qatar became its own market on 1 July 2026, Kuwait and Oman on 1 October 2026; Bahrain stays in "Rest of Middle East". Each shop uses its own number and its own Meta verification (ALY-39).
- Recommendation, in four stages with three gates: Qatar only (CR, then 10 paying shops; build a country and VAT field per shop, prices per currency, Meta prices per market) → gate: 10 paying Qatar shops and the lawyer's answers on Kuwait → Kuwait, sold remotely through founder trips and one Kuwaiti reseller on commission → gate: Kuwait shops renew for a second month → UAE, sold remotely, VAT registered → gate: revenue can fund a Saudi company, or a Saudi partner or paying pilot appears → Saudi Arabia with a Saudi company. Bahrain and Oman take referrals.
- Nine sets of questions for the lawyer and tax adviser, added to ALY-32, must be answered before the first sale outside Qatar (CR activity list, ownership, Kuwait, Saudi, UAE, Oman, Bahrain, privacy, resellers). Points marked unverified come from secondary sources.
- Sources it cited: PayPro Global, Avalara, vatcalc and VATupdate (VAT); Wafeq, KPMG and Comarch (e-invoicing); Dariba, Stackcue and Gulf News (withholding and income tax); EY, Orbitax and PwC (treaties); BDO, US Trade.gov, Al Tamimi, CST, Mondaq and Arab Future (licensing); Clyde & Co, Modulos, CMS and Chambers 2026 (privacy); Gulf News, Mubasher and Forbes Middle East (payments); Meta's WhatsApp pricing page. It notes none of them is legal advice, and that sources disagree on whether Oman's privacy regulation has been issued.
